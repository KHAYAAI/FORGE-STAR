The build plan, layer by layer

1. System architecture: what the satellite actually is

Start with a PocketQube or 1U–3U CubeSat-class demonstrator, not a bespoke bus. The cost data is unambiguous: a 1U CubeSat with COTS/open-source parts runs $5,000–$50,000 in hardware, and a PocketQube can be built for under €30,000 total including launch. A single open-source CubeSat with a LoRa transceiver can be built for under $5,000 and launched for $30,000–$100,000.

Your first vehicle should carry:

Flight computer: NASA cFS or F´ on a COTS ARM-based OBC (designed or iterated in heypcb.ai).
EPS: MPPT + battery + distribution, automotive-grade where possible.
Radio: LoRa/UHF or S-band for the first link; do not attempt a phased array on flight one.
ADCS electronics: magnetorquer drivers, sun sensors, basic attitude determination.
Payload interface: a simple store-and-forward IoT/messaging payload.
This is exactly the envelope where heypcb.ai is genuinely useful: OBC, EPS, radio, ADCS, and payload-interface boards are all PCB-level problems it can accelerate. Structure, thermal, deployment mechanisms, and high-performance RF remain outside its scope and must be handled with mechanical CAD and dedicated RF tools.

2. Spectrum and ITU: the real critical path

This is where most plans die, and it must start before you build much hardware.

Exosat’s claimed position—an 11,000-satellite ITU filing, described as the largest outside the US and China—is the single most valuable asset in its story. You cannot replicate that quickly. What you can do is understand the deadline structure and file early through a neutral administration (Singapore, Luxembourg, UAE).

The rules are rigid:

Milestone	Deadline after bringing-into-use period	Deployment required
Milestone 1	2 years	10% of notified constellation
Milestone 2	5 years	50%
Milestone 3	7 years	100%
Miss a milestone and the ITU does not cancel you—it automatically reduces your filing to a multiple of what you actually deployed. File for 10,000 and deploy 400 by Milestone 1, and you keep a filing for 4,000, not 10,000. The “bring into use” period itself is seven years from the complete coordination request, and for NGSO a single satellite maintained for 90 continuous days in a notified orbital plane can satisfy the BIU requirement—but the milestones then bite hard.

Implication for your plan: do not file a mega-constellation number you cannot plausibly begin deploying. A smaller, honest filing that you can actually populate is worth more than a paper constellation that gets truncated at the first milestone. Your spectrum strategy should be: (a) secure a national administration willing to file on your behalf, (b) coordinate for the specific bands you need first (L/S for IoT/D2C, Ka/Ku for broadband later), and (c) build your launch and manufacturing cadence backwards from the milestone deadlines.

3. Manufacturing and supply chain: the ITAR-free bet

The core Exosat thesis is that sub-500 km orbits plus an East Asian automotive-grade supply chain with zero ITAR/EAR content enables a ~$300K satellite** versus a **~$5M industry standard, with a 3–5 year lifespan. This is a real and coherent cost thesis, not a fantasy—SpaceX itself has moved toward commercial silicon and automotive-grade parts with architectural redundancy rather than single-part hardness.

But the execution gap is large. “Automotive-grade” does not mean “space-qualified.” You still need:

Radiation tolerance: automotive parts will latch up or degrade in LEO without mitigation. You need architectural redundancy, watchdog recovery, and selective shielding—not just COTS chips.
Thermal-vacuum and vibration qualification for every board and structure.
A manufacturing process that produces identical units at volume, not one-off lab builds.
Supplier contracts that guarantee zero US-origin controlled content across solar panels, batteries, thrusters, antennas, and processors.
The practical build sequence is: build one, qualify it brutally, then design for volume. Do not attempt high-volume manufacturing until you have flight heritage on the bus and payload.

4. Launch: diversify from day one

Your ITAR-free design enables launch on non-US providers, which is the entire point of the strategy. The 2026 rideshare market gives you real options:

SpaceX rideshare: ~$7,000/kg to SSO/LEO (but US-controlled manifest and ITAR-adjacent risk).
ISRO PSLV/SSLV: reliable rideshare workhorse, competitively priced for international payloads.
PocketQube-specific: Alba Orbital launch opportunities starting around €25,000.
European providers: Isar Aerospace, Vega-C, and others positioning as sovereign alternatives to SpaceX and PSLV.
For the first demonstrator, a PocketQube rideshare or a 1U/3U rideshare slot is the fastest and cheapest path to orbit. For the commercial constellation, you need a launch cadence plan that is not SpaceX-gated—otherwise you inherit the same bottleneck Exosat is designed to avoid.

Judgement: where this plan is strong, and where it breaks

What is genuinely strong

The cost thesis is coherent and timely. The combination of lower orbits, automotive-grade supply chains, and ITAR-free design is a real architectural bet that attacks a real bottleneck. It is not hand-waving—it mirrors what SpaceX has done with commercial silicon and redundancy, applied to a different market segment.

The open-source + heypcb.ai stack de-risks the early technical phase substantially. You can get to a functional electronics prototype and a flight-ready CubeSat demonstrator far faster and cheaper than a traditional aerospace program. This is enough to attract talent, partners, and seed capital.

The market gap is real. There is a genuine political demand for a third option between Starlink and Chinese state constellations, and the incumbents cannot easily serve it because of their own geopolitical constraints.

Where it breaks

Spectrum is the binding constraint, not technology. The ITU milestone structure is unforgiving. If you file for a large constellation and cannot deploy 10% within two years of the BIU period, your filing is automatically truncated. Exosat’s 11,000-satellite filing is an extraordinary asset, but it also creates an extraordinary execution obligation. A plan that treats spectrum as a background task rather than the primary schedule driver is a plan that will fail regardless of how good the electronics are.

“ITAR-free at scale” is much harder than “ITAR-free on a prototype.” Building one board with no US-origin controlled parts is achievable. Building thousands of identical satellites with a guaranteed clean supply chain across solar, battery, propulsion, and RF—while hitting a $300K unit cost—is a manufacturing and supplier-development problem that will take years and significant capital. The open-source stack does not solve this. heypcb.ai does not solve this.

LOIs are not contracts, and government demand is slower than technical progress. The plan’s commercial validation rests on LOIs and pilots. The real test is whether a national operator or ministry will commit binding capital before you have a working commercial service. The co-financing structure Exosat uses is the right answer, but it is also much harder to sell than a standard vendor contract.

The full constellation is a multi-billion-dollar program that no early-stage venture can fund alone. This is why the phased, customer-financed, sovereign-partnership model is the only credible path. If the plan implicitly assumes that a venture round will carry you to hundreds of satellites, it is mispriced.

The honest bottom line

This is a buildable plan, but only if you sequence it correctly. What they do not do is change the physics of the business: spectrum deadlines, manufacturing qualification, launch cadence, and government sales cycles are the schedule, not the engineering. If your plan is organized around “build the satellite first, then figure out spectrum and customers,” it is backwards. If it is organized around “secure the spectrum pathway and anchor customer financing, then use the open-source/PCB stack to build the minimum viable vehicle that satisfies them,” it has a real chance.

The plan is neither naive nor guaranteed. The open-source stack, layer by layer

Flight software — the satellite's brain

We standardise on NASA F´ (F Prime) as the primary flight software framework, with NASA cFS as the production-grade alternative for missions that require a more traditional architecture. F´ is a component-driven C++ framework developed at JPL and already deployed on multiple space missions; it is explicitly “tailored but not limited to small-scale spaceflight systems such as CubeSats, SmallSats, and instruments”. It provides discrete software components with well-defined interfaces, core message-queue and threading capabilities, modelling tools that auto-generate code, and unit/integration testing tools. It is free, open source, and flight-proven.

Inter-subsystem communication uses the CubeSat Space Protocol (CSP), a lightweight network-layer delivery protocol designed specifically for CubeSats.

Ground segment and mission control

SatNOGS is the backbone of the ground network: a global, open-source, participatory network of SDR-based ground stations that supports telemetry collection and satellite observation through community participation. It is licensed under AGPLv3/GPLv3 for software and CERN OHL for hardware, and a station can be built around an RTL-SDR receiver, a Raspberry Pi, and a VHF/UHF antenna. This gives FORGE Star immediate access to distributed ground coverage at near-zero capital cost for early operations.

Mission control runs on COSMOS (open-source command and control and satellite test framework) with Open MCT (NASA’s next-generation mission control visualisation framework) for dashboards and telemetry display. TinyGS provides an ultra-low-cost LoRa-based global network for narrowband IoT and telemetry, using cheap, versatile modules. Yamcs is available as an alternative mission-control backend that also works with Open MCT.

Constellation simulation and network emulation

Before committing capital to hardware, we simulate the constellation at high fidelity. OpenSN is the primary emulator: a container-based open-source library for large-scale LEO satellite networks that allows distributed routing software to run on each node and scales horizontally across multiple machines. It constructs mega-constellations 5–10× faster than StarryNet and updates link state 2–4× faster than LeoEM. LeoEM (Mininet-based) and StarPerf (flow-level) are used for complementary routing and capacity studies.

5G / Non-Terrestrial Network (NTN) stack — the Direct-to-Cell path

For Direct-to-Cell and 3GPP-compliant satellite access, we use OpenAirInterface (OAI) with its NTN extensions. OAI’s NTN support was developed jointly by Fraunhofer IIS, Allbesmart, and the University of Luxembourg, and supports both GEO and LEO transparent satellites — the base station signal is sent from the ground, received and amplified by the satellite, and sent back to the user equipment on Earth. OAI’s RAN is compatible with free5GC and Open5GS as open-source 5G core networks. This gives FORGE Star a fully open, SDR-based path to prototyping end-to-end 5G NTN without proprietary RAN licences.

Orbit determination, propagation, and mission design

Orekit (Java, with Python wrappers via orbdetpy) is the industry-standard open-source flight dynamics library for orbit determination and propagation. GMAT (NASA’s General Mission Analysis Tool) handles trajectory optimisation and mission analysis. poliastro provides Python-native astrodynamics for rapid iteration, and Skyfield handles TLE propagation and precise time handling. Nyx is available as a high-fidelity Rust/Python astrodynamics toolkit if higher performance is needed.

RF, SDR, and signal processing

GNU Radio with gr-satellites and SatDump form the RF prototyping and decoding stack. SoapySDR provides vendor-neutral SDR hardware abstraction. This is sufficient to design, test, and fly narrowband IoT and basic telemetry links without proprietary RF tooling.

Electronics design — heypcb.ai

heypcb.ai is the AI-native PCB design accelerator. It combines AI-assisted schematic capture, component placement, and routing with Circuit World, a browser-based library of 8,800+ open-hardware boards that engineers can fork and edit with an AI agent, exporting real KiCad projects, Gerbers, BOMs, and pick-and-place files. For FORGE Star, heypcb.ai handles the on-board computer (OBC), electrical power system (EPS), radio/telemetry boards, ADCS electronics, and payload interface boards. It does not design the structure, thermal system, solar deployment mechanisms, propulsion hardware, or high-performance phased arrays — those require mechanical CAD, thermal analysis, and specialised RF design tools.

What it costs to build

The first demonstrator: PocketQube or 1U–3U CubeSat

The fastest, cheapest path to orbit is a PocketQube or 1U–3U CubeSat rideshare. A 1U CubeSat slot on a LEO rideshare costs $5,500–$10,000 all-in in 2026, with SpaceX Transporter often the cheapest option; a 3U slot runs $12,000–$28,000 depending on orbit. PocketQube launch services are even cheaper — roughly €25,000 for a 1p PocketQube, compared with approximately $100,000 for a 1U and $300,000 for a 3U through traditional CubeSat channels.

Total mission cost including integration, testing, and licensing is typically $20,000–$40,000 for a 1U and $40,000–$80,000+ for a 3U, once deployer adapters, vibration and TVAC testing, EMI/EMC testing, RF licensing, and launch-site travel are included. The satellite hardware itself — OBC, EPS, radio, ADCS, structure, and payload — can be built for $5,000–$50,000 using open-source CubeSat references and heypcb.ai-designed boards.

Spectrum and regulatory costs

The ICASA pathway is not free, but it is tractable. A satellite operator serving South Africa requires an Individual Electronic Communications Service (I-ECS) licence, an Individual Electronic Communications Network Service (I-ECNS) licence, and Radio Frequency Spectrum (RFS) licence(s) . The RFS application fee is set under the Radio Frequency Spectrum Regulations, and annual RFS usage fees apply thereafter. RF licensing alone (ITU/FCC) is budgeted at $5,000–$25,000 with a 6–18 month timeline for a single mission. A full NGSO constellation filing through ICASA is a larger administrative undertaking, but the ITU cost-recovery structure is defined and bounded: the ITU charges cost-recovery fees for processing satellite network filings, with a unit-based formula that scales with filing complexity. The real cost is administrative capacity and time, not a prohibitive filing fee.

Manufacturing cost at scale — the $300k target

The commercial thesis targets **~$300,000 per satellite** at volume, against a traditional $5M+ benchmark. Industry data supports the direction of travel: analyst consensus for Starlink satellite build cost is approximately $0.5–1M per satellite**, with implied **$2,000–$4,000 per kg**; a technology-demonstration CubeSat typically costs **$50,000 per kg, while an operational mission runs $300,000 per kg. FORGE Star’s target is aggressive but not implausible for a 50–100 kg-class satellite built with automotive-grade parts, architectural redundancy instead of single-part radiation hardness, and an ITAR-free supply chain anchored partly in South Africa’s automotive components base and partly in East Asian volume manufacturing.

South Africa already has relevant industrial depth: one in five satellites orbiting Earth contains a part made in the Western Cape, and local firms such as CubeSpace, Simera Sense, and Cubecom design and manufacture satellite components, subsystems, and complete spacecraft. A satellite supplier park exists in Silverton. The automotive supply chain is a genuine anchor, not a metaphor.

How we price what we sell

The IoT market has a wide price dispersion. FORGE Star’s pricing should sit in the $2–10 per device per month range for standard messaging plans, with premium tiers for higher message frequency or lower latency. Direct-to-Cell — wholesale to mobile operators

D2C is not a retail consumer product; it is wholesale capacity sold to mobile network operators, who bundle it into their existing tariffs. The wholesale D2C market was valued at $3.8 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $47.2 billion by 2034 at a 35% CAGR. FORGE Star’s pricing to MNOs should be structured as per-GB wholesale rates or per-subscriber monthly fees, positioned below Starlink’s D2C wholesale economics. Starlink’s D2C service is live with T-Mobile and multiple international partners, and the pattern is consistent: operators use satellite D2C for coverage in dead zones they could never afford to build out terrestrially. FORGE Star sells the same value proposition — coverage extension without terrestrial capex — but with sovereign control and local co-financing.

Sovereign constellations — the core commercial differentiator

The largest revenue line is turnkey sovereign constellations: a government or national operator leases capacity, co-finances a dedicated constellation, or buys and operates a network built with FORGE Star satellites. This is priced as a multi-year capacity or network contract, typically structured as upfront financing plus revenue share with the local partner. This shifts part of the constellation capex off FORGE Star’s balance sheet and onto the customer who wants sovereign control — which is precisely the customer segment willing to pay for neutrality. The reference point is not a per-GB tariff; it is the cost of dependence on a US or Chinese system, which sovereign buyers are explicitly trying to avoid.
